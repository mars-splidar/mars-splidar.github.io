    Motivation

    Why fast, accurate simulation is hard

    Training networks needs abundant histogram cubes. The sequential gold standard examines every photon with Monte Carlo rejection sampling, then repeats that per pixel and per random realization — it cannot scale in flux, resolution, or dataset size.

    Our approach

    An analytical simulator

    The temporal shape of a registered histogram was already solved. We solve the missing piece — the photon count — in closed form, enabling direct synthesis across every pixel at once.

    Our contributions

    The theory

    The first analytic photon-count law under dead time.

    • Closed-form mean and variance via a CLT

    Theory

    Theory

    Dead time is not a nuisance — it destroys independence between detections, which is why the registered photon count is not Poisson and why standard renewal theory does not rescue it. Treating registration as a Markov-renewal process is what makes the count tractable again.

    Method

    Counting is the inverse of waiting

    N(t) → 𝒩( t / μ, t · σ² / μ³ ) The photon count N of t converges to a normal distribution whose mean is t divided by mu and whose variance is t times sigma squared divided by mu cubed.

    Over an exposure of length t, the registered count converges to a Gaussian. Its mean is the exposure divided by the average wait between registrations; its variance follows from how that wait varies. Both have closed forms, and that is what makes a precomputed table possible at all.

    The embedded Markov chain says where the next detection lands; the holding time says when it happens on the absolute clock. Because the chain mixes quickly, the total elapsed time obeys a central limit theorem — and counting is the inverse of waiting, so the count inherits a Gaussian law with closed-form mean and variance.

    Consecutive waits are correlated, so the variance carries a long tail of covariance terms. The tail decays geometrically — but the decay pattern swings with signal, background and dead time, so no fixed cutoff works. A spectral truncation rule sums the remainder instead, reaching a mean absolute error of 10.00 against 125.91 for the best fixed cutoff: 12× closer.

    Results

    Does the theory hold up?

    KL to ground truth: Poisson 24.208, Renewal 18.821, Zhang 0.409, MaRS 0.013 — 30× closer than the best prior model.

    Registered count distributions at low and at high flux, including the variance failure mode of the best prior model.

    Validated on a real SPAD: 85 ns dead time, 7.5 MHz laser, TV distance 0.065 (bright) and 0.060 (dark) against measured histograms.

    Simulator

    Simulator

    A count law only pays off if a full histogram cube can be rendered from it. MaRS precomputes the closed forms into a lookup table, then samples every pixel of a cube at once — no per-photon loop anywhere in the path.

    Method

    From closed-form equations to a rendered cube

    Batched lookup, temporal alignment, then multinomial sampling — fully vectorized across every pixel, with no per-photon loop.

    A table too big to store — until now

    A naive lookup table over signal, background, and depth is 108 GB. Delay invariance removes depth entirely: 105 MB.

    The invariance holds at a fixed signal and background level: with those held, moving the delay leaves the count’s mean and variance untouched and only shifts the temporal shape around the laser period. So one table is built per sensor configuration — laser period, bin count, pulse width and dead time — and then reused for every pixel and every depth. The tables behind this page’s model figures use a simulation grid at 75 ns dead time.

    Results

    Does it deliver in practice?

    Runtime stays nearly flat as flux, realizations, and pixel count scale up — the sequential simulator does not.

    1.013 s ± 0.071 s per cube versus 40.831 s ± 8.045 s for the gold standard, on a 128×128×1024 histogram cube — a measured 40×.

    Utility

    Utility: accurate counts, better depth and reflectivity recovery

    Two things follow from getting the count right. The first is the precomputability test: several simulators are individually accurate, but only one is both accurate and precomputable into a table fast enough to train on. The second is count-model isolation: pair the same proven temporal shape with different count models and the count model is the only thing left varying, so whatever happens downstream can only be coming from it.

    Correct statistics, or practical speed — until now, not both

    A capability matrix across five simulators shows which ones get the mean, variance, and temporal PDF right, and which ones can be precomputed into a lookup table fast enough for training. MaRS is the only row with every checkmark.

    Does the count model alone make the difference?

    Pairing an accurate temporal PDF with different count models isolates the effect of the count model itself on unbiased estimation and transfer to gold-standard test data.

    The renewal and Zhang et al. rows below carry the same accurate temporal shape and differ only in their count model, so the distance between a row’s two columns can only be coming from the count model.

    Limitations

    Assumes a nonparalyzable detector and negligible afterpulsing. Paralyzable detectors, timing jitter, and afterpulsing are left for future work.

    The Markov-renewal framework itself applies to any known transient arrival rate whose induced chain mixes, so non-Gaussian pulses and multiple returns are in scope in principle.
